Should Inito build a perimenopause product?

1

The business opportunity

The menopause-related market is projected to reach roughly $24.35B by 2030 (5.4% CAGR).[14] One widely cited estimate puts the broader femtech category near $97B by 2030.[15] More informative than total size is where competing capital has gone:

CategoryPlayersStrong atCan't do
HRT telehealth & prescribingMidi Health[5], Evernow[6], Alloy[7], Winona[8]Care delivery, prescribingFrequent, objective measurement
One-time test kitsHertility Health[9], myLabBox[10]A single lab-grade snapshotShowing a fluctuating, multi-week process
WearablesApple Watch, WhoopPassive, always-onMeasuring hormones directly
Symptom trackersFlo[12], Balance[13]Capturing what she feelsAn objective anchor for why

No player currently combines frequent, direct, affordable hormone measurement with personalised interpretation over time. That gap — not the size of the market — is the argument for a perimenopause product specifically.

The one open technical question Our assay and reader calibration were built and validated for reproductive-age cycling hormone levels, to confirm ovulation. Perimenopause produces excursions outside that range — sharply elevated, erratic FSH during anovulatory cycles; unpredictable estrogen spikes and crashes. Whether the existing assay is accurate across that wider range, or whether this product is gated on new antibody development, is genuinely unresolved. Working assumption for the rest of this document: adequate but not optimal — good enough to validate the product now, improving as we iterate on the go. If that's wrong, the design still holds; only the timeline moves.
2

User type

U
"The Unexplained"

Early-to-mid perimenopause, still cycling but irregularly. Multiple disruptive symptoms — sleep, mood, hot flashes, cycle chaos. Has seen a doctor once or not at all; if she has, got an ambiguous or "normal" single-test result, because one visit can't catch a fluctuating process. Most plausibly sourced from our own trying-to-conceive (TTC) previous base who has aged in — near-zero incremental acquisition cost, and she already trusts our hormone claims from her fertility journey.

Not targeted first: women whose job-to-be-done is pure symptom relief — a pharmacology problem HRT telehealth already serves better — and late/post-menopausal women, whose flattened hormones leave little week-to-week signal for the core mechanic to work with.

3

First feature / experience

The smallest thing that can turn "I don't know if this is hormonal" into "here's what I've learned about my own body": measure → log → build a personal baseline → surface a confidence-scored insight → suggest one concrete action. Internally, the Pattern Journal.

Not built in v1: a large hormone panel, HRT telehealth or prescribing, a community/content platform, or a live hormone dashboard. Each is a legitimate later direction; none is why we have the right to win on day one, and each adds regulatory, clinical, or content scope this case hasn't validated yet.

Avenue to exploreWearable integration (Apple Watch, Whoop) is a plausible v2 direction, not a v1 one. A wearable adds continuous, passive signal between test days — resting heart rate, skin temperature, sleep staging — which could tighten the estrogen-swing-to-symptom association the Pattern Journal already surfaces from hormone readings and self-reported logs. But it doesn't replace the hormone measurement: an elevated overnight temperature or a disrupted sleep stage looks the same whether the cause is a hormone shift, a fever, travel, or a bad night's sleep, and only the hormone reading disambiguates that. So the honest role for wearable data here is as another confounder input feeding the same confidence-scored model, not a new headline metric — worth revisiting once the core hormone-symptom association is validated on self-reported logs alone, not before.
4

What's changing, and what she feels

A multi-year transition — typically 4–8 years[20] — driven by three hormones moving at once: estrogen swings erratically (often high before it's low) before eventually declining, progesterone softens early and then drops sharply once ovulation becomes infrequent, and FSH climbs as the ovary needs a louder signal to respond.

Hormone trend across the transition

Schematic, relative to each hormone's own reproductive-age baseline — not a plot of lab units, which vary widely by woman and by day.[20]
Individual timelines vary by years in either direction. Estrogen often holds steady or even rises before it swings and falls — a single low reading early on doesn't mean much. Those late-perimenopause swings are typically the least predictable part of the pattern — exactly why single-test snapshots struggle here.

5

The mechanism, not just correlation

Estrogen → hot flashes, end to end: estrogen normally restrains kisspeptin/neurokinin-B/dynorphin (KNDy) neuron activity in the hypothalamus.[16] As estrogen withdraws or swings erratically, KNDy neurons become hyperactive and narrow the body's thermoneutral zone, so small, normal core-temperature fluctuations trigger a full heat-dissipation response — flushing, sweating, vasodilation — which in turn fragments sleep. This pathway is well-characterised enough to underpin an FDA-approved non-hormonal drug for hot flashes (fezolinetant, an NK3-receptor antagonist).[17]

Progesterone → mood & sleep, end to end: progesterone's metabolite allopregnanolone is a positive modulator of GABA-A receptors — the body's own calming/sedative system.[18] As perimenopausal cycles become increasingly anovulatory or short-luteal, progesterone (and allopregnanolone) drops or becomes erratic, reducing that calming effect — plausibly producing irritability, anxiety, or lighter sleep. This pathway, too, is well-characterised enough to underpin an FDA-approved drug: zuranolone, a synthetic allopregnanolone analog and GABA-A modulator, was approved in 2023 as the first oral treatment for postpartum depression.[25] That trial evidence is for postpartum depression, not perimenopausal mood change specifically, so the read-across here is inferred from a shared mechanism, not directly proven for this population — and individual sensitivity to the shift varies substantially, which is why the pattern must be discovered per user rather than asserted universally.

6

Guarding against correlation vs. causation

An explicit evidence ladder governs what the product is allowed to say, and nothing higher than the evidence supports.

LevelEvidenceWhat the product may say
1Population science"Research links estrogen changes to hot flashes in many women."
2Biological plausibility"There's a plausible reason lower progesterone could relate to sleep and mood."
3User-specific association"In your data, X and Y have occurred together N times." (requires ≥3 cycles / ~8–10 weeks of data)
4Temporal relationship"Y tends to follow X within [a stated window]." (ordering, not causation)
5Repeated within-person pattern"This has repeated across N of your last M cycles — more than expected by chance at your own baseline." (our strongest safe claim)
6Intervention-responseNot attempted in v1 — requires a controlled n-of-1 test.

Confounders to account for

Sleep debt, stress, diet, exercise, alcohol · medication/HRT use · illness, travel, life events · measurement noise · regression to the mean · individual baseline differences.

Language, by design

Uses: may relate to · has coincided with · in your data · not enough data yet

Never: "this is causing…" · "your hormones are the reason…" · "you have [condition]" · "this proves…"

Product behavior when patterns don't line up

SituationBehavior
Pattern detectedShow the confidence-scored insight, sample size, and counter-evidence. Offer one concrete action.
Weak / early pattern"Early signal, not enough data yet to be confident" — invites continued tracking rather than asserting or hiding it.
No pattern"Your hot flashes don't appear to track closely with your estrogen readings so far — that's useful to know," then redirects to non-hormonal candidates.
ContradictionNever let a number overrule lived experience: "Your readings didn't show the change we'd expect. That doesn't mean it isn't real."
Insufficient dataExplicit "still learning your baseline" for the first 4–6 weeks — never fabricates a pattern to seem useful early.
Potential red flagBypasses the pattern-confidence system entirely. Immediate, unhedged medical-escalation language.

9

Wireframes

Nine screens — the minimum set that proves the product logic, not visual polish.

1. Onboarding

Route her correctly; screen out red flags and non-cycling users.

2. Daily check-in

Capture symptoms in under 20 seconds — tap icons, no typing.

3. Measurement

Same urine test strip & reader as TTC, but once a week year-round instead of through a fertile window.

4. Weekly insight

One idea per week, confidence tier always visible.

5. What this may mean

Show the reasoning, including counter-evidence.

6. Symptom timeline

Give her the months of memory a doctor visit needs.

7. No clear pattern

Make "no pattern" feel useful, not a dead end.

8. What you can do

Every insight ends with a concrete next step.

9. Doctor summary

A 2-minute, evidence-based appointment opener.
